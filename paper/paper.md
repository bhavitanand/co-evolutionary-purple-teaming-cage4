TITLE: LLM-Written Detection Rules Are Quiet but Incomplete: A Pilot in CAGE-4 with Negative Results on Co-Evolutionary Purple Teaming

ABSTRACT: Detection rules are the working material of a security operations centre (SOC), and they age quickly because attackers change their behaviour once a rule starts to fire. We ask whether a team of language-model agents can write and maintain Sigma-style detection rules in a closed loop with an adaptive attacker. On the CAGE Challenge 4 enterprise network we built a telemetry bridge that turns simulator observations into rule-matchable events with hidden ground-truth labels, five LLM Blue agents that propose validated rules every round, and a Red agent that adapts its plan to how much of its activity was caught. Frozen rule sets from ten training runs per condition were scored on ten held-out seeds against five Red strategies, one of them unseen. The LLM-written rules raise few false alarms (false-positive rate of at most 0.03) but are incomplete: native recall is 0.49 for the co-evolved system and 0.26 against a fixed Red. Co-evolution is the hypothesis we tested and did not confirm: its recall advantage over a fixed attacker did not replicate on ten further runs per condition (+0.076 over all twenty, with an interval that includes zero), and neither a scripted evasive Red nor a larger rule budget changed that. Performance feedback traded recall for precision. Pooling the rules of independent runs raises recall steadily (0.69, 0.82 and 0.96 for three, five and ten runs, at false-positive rates of 0.024, 0.045 and 0.080), and every test episode is detected within a median of 0 to 6.5 steps. A baseline check shows that the testbed is easy to separate (attackers are 84% of native events, so raw precision is flattered): a greedy learner with single-field rules reaches recall 0.94 to 1.00 at no false positives, whereas the LLM rules wrap the same discriminating port in narrow, analyst-style context and lean on fields our bridge filled in (blanking them cuts co-evolved recall from 0.49 to 0.19). We report the trade-offs, an event-level account of what the rules miss, and the negative results as a reproducible pilot with released code, rule sets and test episodes.

# Introduction
Detection engineers write rules that turn raw telemetry into alerts, and attackers then change their behaviour until the rules stop firing. Keeping up with that loop is slow and manual: rules are written by experts, need constant maintenance, and differ between SIEM platforms [5], [6], while attacker techniques are catalogued in frameworks such as MITRE ATT&CK [17]. Large language models (LLMs) can already draft detection rules from threat reports [5]-[8], and they can act as defenders in simulated networks [2], but these studies evaluate the defender against attacks that do not react to it. A rule set that looks strong on a fixed trace can fail against an attacker who has seen what the rules catch.
Purple teaming addresses this in practice by putting the attacking and defending teams into one feedback loop. This paper asks whether a small version of that loop can be automated end to end. An LLM-driven Red agent adapts its plan to what the defender detects, and LLM-driven Blue agents revise executable, human-readable detection rules from what they missed and what they flagged wrongly. We build the loop on CAGE Challenge 4 (CAGE-4) [9], built on the CybORG gym [23], a public enterprise-network simulation, and evaluate frozen rule sets on held-out seeds and on an attacker strategy never seen in training.
The study is deliberately a pilot. It uses a 4-billion-parameter local model, ten training runs of eight rounds per condition, and a detection-only defender whose rules raise alerts but do not yet change the simulated network. Part of the telemetry is synthetic, because CAGE-4 does not log what Sigma-style rules need to match on. We therefore treat the results as measurements of a method on a testbed, not as evidence about production SOCs, and we report native and synthetic events separately throughout.
We organise the evaluation around six research questions.
RQ1 (validity and precision). Can LLM agents write valid, precise Sigma-style rules from telemetry alone?
RQ2 (coverage). How much of the attack do their rules detect, compared with hand-written and random rule sets?
RQ3 (feedback and co-evolution). Does performance feedback, or training against an adaptive Red instead of a fixed one, improve the rules?
RQ4 (adaptation). Does the LLM Red planner respond to the defender, and does a stronger, evasion-rewarded Red change the answer to RQ3?
RQ5 (robustness). Do the conclusions hold for a larger model and for a larger rule budget?
RQ6 (baselines). Do rule sets fitted without an LLM match the LLM-written rules, and how easy is the testbed?
Contributions:
(1) A round-based co-evolution loop in which an LLM planner drives an adaptive Red agent and five LLM Blue agents write validated Sigma-style rules once per round, with cheap executors handling individual simulation steps (Section IV).
(2) A telemetry bridge that converts CAGE-4's native observations into rule-matchable events, with every event tagged native, enriched, or synthetic so that the share of invented telemetry is reported, and with ground-truth labels attached at the moment the simulator creates the underlying record (Section IV-B).
(3) A pilot evaluation of frozen rule sets on held-out seeds and an unseen Red strategy, with six conditions, a no-feedback ablation, bootstrap differences between conditions, and a breakdown of missed attacker events by action and event type (Section VI).
(4) An exploratory finding, replicated on twenty runs per condition, that pooling the rules of independent runs raises recall at a false-positive rate well below that of hand-written rules (Section VI-F), and a set of negative and mixed findings that bound what such a loop can claim: feedback buys precision at the cost of recall, the LLM Red planner's plans follow the round number, and a scripted evasive Red does not show a benefit of co-evolution (Sections VI and VIII).
(5) A baseline check: rule sets fitted without an LLM reach recall 0.94 to 1.00 at no false positives because attackers are 84% of native test events (Section VI-G). Campaign-level detection is in the main results: every rule set flags every test episode within a few steps (Section VI-B).
# Related Work
*Rule generation with LLMs.* RulePilot turns an analyst's description into SIEM queries through an agent with an intermediate representation and a reflection step [5]. AutoSigma and LLMCloudHunter extract Sigma-style rules from threat reports [6], [7], and ThreatPilot additionally generates attack commands to check the rules against [8]. CTI-REALM benchmarks agents on rule generation against real telemetry [10]. These systems start from a report and produce rules once. None of them faces an attacker that responds to the rules.
*LLM and co-evolutionary agents on CAGE-4.* Castro et al. use LLMs as CAGE-4 defenders that choose actions, with a scripted Red [2]. Hemberg et al. evolve Red and Blue team controllers with grammatical evolution and an LLM-supported mutation operator, and find that two-sided co-evolution lowers both the highs and the lows of each side's performance and induces fluctuations [1]. Their agents output game actions rather than rules, and they list out-of-sample evaluation as future work. Shaikh et al. build an LLM-plus-RL Red agent against CAGE-4 defenders and show that stand-alone LLM attackers struggle to sustain multi-stage campaigns [3].
*Red-versus-Blue loops with LLMs.* Several recent systems pair an attacker and a defender around LLMs themselves. PAD alternates an attacker and a defender to align models against jailbreaks [4]. AutoPT frames purple teaming for LLM security as an autonomous red and blue loop with coverage-guided exploration [12], BlueCodeAgent lets automated red teaming feed a blue-teaming agent for code generation [13], Co-RedTeam orchestrates multi-agent vulnerability discovery and exploitation [14], and ACEA connects red and blue LLM systems in a head-to-head arena [15]. Du describes LLM Red and Blue agents that improve through prompt augmentation in a small testbed [11]. These works target model safety, code security, or exploitation. Our setting is network defence, and our Blue artefact is an inspectable rule set that can be scored event by event.
*Co-evolution.* Competitive co-evolution has a long history in evolutionary computation. Hillis showed that co-evolving parasites improve an optimisation procedure [20], cooperative co-evolution splits a problem across populations [21], and well-known failure modes such as disengagement and cycling are part of that literature. We borrow the vocabulary but not the machinery: our two sides alternate once per round and learn through prompts and a retirement rule, not through populations.
*Position.* We combine an adaptive Red, LLM-written executable rules, hidden ground-truth scoring, and evaluation of frozen rules on held-out seeds and an unseen attacker, inside one simulator. Each ingredient exists in prior work; we are not aware of a study that evaluates them together, although our literature check was not a systematic review.
# Problem Statement and Threat Model
## Problem formulation
An episode produces a stream of events e_1, ..., e_T, each with Blue-visible fields x_t and a hidden binary label y_t that is 1 when the event was caused by Red. A rule set R maps an event to an alert, a(e) = 1 when any rule in R matches x_t. Quality is measured per event:
Precision = TP / (TP + FP), Recall = TP / (TP + FN), FPR = FP / (FP + TN),
where TP counts alerts on Red-caused events, FP alerts on benign events, FN missed Red-caused events, and TN silent benign events. A Blue team improves its rule set over rounds r = 1, ..., 8 using feedback computed from the labels on training episodes only. The final rule set is frozen and evaluated on held-out test seeds without any feedback. The question is how recall, precision and FPR of the frozen set depend on how the rules were produced.
## Threat model and assumptions
Red can take the ten CAGE-4 Red actions on the simulated enterprise network (discovery, exploitation, privilege escalation, impact, degradation, withdrawal, and sleep) and chooses a plan once per round. The adaptive Red sees only the share of its activity that was caught in earlier rounds (and, in one condition, the share per action); it never sees the rules. Blue sees only the sixteen visible event fields. Ground-truth labels are used for training feedback and for scoring, and are never part of what Blue or the frozen rules see at test time. We assume labelled training episodes, as they would exist in a purple-team exercise, and we assume the simulated telemetry is a fair stand-in for the fields a SOC would match on, which we cannot check.
Out of scope are real-world deployability, analyst workload, adversaries who tamper with the rule pipeline, and any response action by Blue. Blue here is a detector, not a defender that changes the network.
# System Design
## Environment
CAGE-4 simulates an enterprise network of several security zones. Five Blue agents defend it (four hold one subnet each, the fifth holds three), Green agents generate benign work, and Red agents try to reach and disrupt servers. Red's action space has ten actions: network and service discovery (DiscoverRemoteSystems, AggressiveServiceDiscovery, StealthServiceDiscovery), DiscoverDeception, ExploitRemoteService, PrivilegeEscalate, Impact, DegradeServices, Withdraw, and Sleep. An episode is 500 steps. Blue's native observation is a set of per-host records, not log lines, so a rule language needs an extra layer on top of it. Fig. 2 gives an overview of the loop.
## Telemetry bridge
The bridge converts Blue's native observations into events with sixteen Blue-visible fields (event category, process and parent process, privilege, source and destination address, destination port, protocol, service name, decoy flag, host, subnet, and zone). We measured on 1,500 steps of passive-Blue episodes which Red actions leave a native record. Only ExploitRemoteService and the two service-discovery actions do. PrivilegeEscalate, Impact, DegradeServices, DiscoverRemoteSystems, and DiscoverDeception leave none.
Every event carries a provenance tag. *Native* events come directly from CAGE-4's records (connections with real addresses and ports, session-creation records, and Green's false alerts). *Enriched* fields, such as process names and the service name for a well-known port, are chosen by a seeded hash because the simulator does not provide them. *Synthetic* events are invented for successful PrivilegeEscalate, Impact, and DegradeServices actions, so that Blue has something to detect; they are delivered to the Blue agent that defends the target host's zone. DiscoverRemoteSystems, DiscoverDeception, and Withdraw stay invisible. Ground-truth labels (whether the event was caused by Red) are attached at the moment CAGE-4 creates the underlying record, and they are never part of the Blue-visible event.

## Rule language and validation
Rules are Sigma-style JSON objects [16] with named selections over the visible fields, a condition built from AND, OR, and NOT, a severity level, a confidence value, and a short rationale. Three field modifiers are allowed (contains, startswith, endswith). A rule judges one event at a time; there are no counts or time windows. A validator rejects rules that use unknown fields, unsupported modifiers, or malformed conditions, and invalid rules are discarded. Constrained JSON decoding keeps most proposals well formed.
## Red agent
Red is an LLM planner with a cheap executor. At the start of a round the planner returns a JSON plan: a goal (for example reconnaissance or impact), a scan style (aggressive, stealthy, or mixed), how much to probe for decoys, whether to prioritise servers, and how aggressively to pursue Impact and DegradeServices. The plan is validated, retried once on error, and replaced by CAGE-4's default scripted behaviour if it stays invalid. A finite-state executor built on CAGE-4's own Red agent compiles the plan into action probabilities and plays all 500 steps, checking each action for legality. The planner sees a memory of its last rounds, including how many of its exploits succeeded and what share of its activity the defender's rules caught, and it is told that the defender learns from what it does.
## Blue agents
Each of the five Blue agents calls the LLM once per round for up to five rules. The prompt contains the rule format, the field vocabulary, three valid example rules, a short statement of what the language cannot express, the agent's current rule titles, and a summary of the previous round. That summary always lists the most common event patterns seen in the agent's zone (unlabelled). In the feedback conditions it also states how many attacker and benign events the rules matched, per-rule precision, the attacker patterns that no rule matched, and the benign patterns that fired alerts. These statements are computed from ground-truth labels, so they are used only on training episodes. An agent keeps at most twelve active rules, and after every round a rule that fired at least three times with fewer than 30% of its alerts on attacker activity is retired.
## Conditions
We compare six rule sources. *Static*: ten hand-written rules applied in every zone. *Random rules*: each round every agent adds five rules built from randomly chosen observed events, with the same retirement step and no LLM. *LLM, fixed Red*: Blue with feedback against CAGE-4's default Red. *LLM, co-evolved, no feedback*: Blue sees telemetry only, against the adaptive Red. *LLM, co-evolved*: Blue with feedback against the adaptive Red. *LLM, co-evolved, per-action signal*: as the previous one, but Red's memory also lists, for each of its own actions, the share of that action's events the defender's rules caught. This condition was added after the first results showed that Red's plans hardly changed (Section VI-D); it tests whether a richer signal makes Red respond. Two further conditions use a scripted evasive Red and are described in Section VI-D.
# Experimental Setup
## Training protocol
Each condition has ten independent training runs (seeds 0 to 9), each with eight rounds. A round is one 500-step episode, preceded by one warm-up episode so that the first round already has telemetry to look at. Red and Blue alternate within a round: Blue proposes, Red plans, the episode is played, and both sides receive their feedback. Training environment seeds are derived by hashing and are at least 1000 apart from the evaluation seeds, apart from round one, which uses seeds 0 to 9, so no training episode coincides with a test episode.
## Evaluation protocol
After training, each run's rules are frozen and scored without feedback on test seeds 100 to 109, which are never used in training. Each rule set is scored on 50 test episodes: five Red strategies times ten seeds. Four strategies are seen in the sense that they are the scripted baseline or variants used when developing the system (the default CAGE-4 Red, aggressive, stealthy, and deception-aware); the fifth, a hit-and-run strategy that withdraws from hosts, is held out and appears only in test. Test events are generated once with a passive Blue and cached, so every condition is scored on identical events.
## Metrics and statistics
Precision, recall, and false-positive rate (FPR) are computed per event against the hidden labels, overall and separately for native and synthetic events. Because synthetic events state their signal in an explicit field, recall on them is easy by construction; we therefore never pool them with native events in a headline number. For each condition we average over test episodes within a run, then report the mean and a 95% percentile-bootstrap interval over runs [19]. Differences between conditions use a bootstrap in which each side is resampled independently. With ten runs the intervals are still wide, and the static rule set has a single run and no interval.
## Models and compute
The main experiments use Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 [18] (4-bit quantised) served by llama.cpp [22] on one laptop GPU (NVIDIA RTX 4050, 6 GB); a confirmatory check uses GLM-4.7-Flash (3-bit quantised, partly offloaded to the CPU) on the same machine. A Blue call takes about 14 seconds and returns valid rules about 94% of the time in isolation. Training rounds in the experiments reported here added up to about 46 process-hours, 14 of them for the larger model (several runs shared the one GPU at a time, so this exceeds wall-clock time), plus test-episode generation and scoring. A training round took about two minutes with the 4-billion-parameter model when four runs shared the GPU, and roughly 3 to 9 minutes with the larger model. Appendix A lists all settings.
# Results
## RQ1: validity and precision
About 91% of proposed rules were valid across the LLM conditions, and the active rule set averaged about 53 rules over the eight rounds (60 at the end of training, the cap of 12 per agent). All three LLM conditions produced precise rule sets. On native events precision was at least 0.97 and mean FPR at most 0.03 on both seen and unseen Red (Tables I and II). The random-rule control, by contrast, fired on about half of benign events (FPR 0.49 on seen Red), and the static rules on about one in five (0.19). ## RQ2: coverage
Recall is where the LLM rule sets fall short. On native events against seen Red strategies, the co-evolved system reached 0.49 [0.40, 0.58], the fixed-Red baseline 0.26 [0.16, 0.36], and the no-feedback ablation 0.67 [0.60, 0.74]. The hand-written rules reached 0.96 and the random rules 0.93, but at the false-positive rates above. *Synthetic events.* Recall on synthetic events was 0.53 for the co-evolved system, 0.73 for the ablation, and 0.61 for the fixed-Red baseline, while the static rules reached 1.00; precision was 1.00 for all conditions. That a four-billion-parameter writer misses nearly half of events that announce themselves (an elevated privilege or a stopped service) suggests that the limit is rule coverage per agent and zone rather than the difficulty of the signal, although we did not test this directly.
*Campaign-level detection.* Event recall understates how reliably an attack is noticed. In all 40 seen test episodes every rule set raised at least one alert on an attacker event. The median delay between the first attacker event and the first alert, averaged over runs, was 0.0 to 0.2 steps for the hand-written, random and no-feedback sets, 2.0 steps for the co-evolved set, 3.5 for the per-action set and 6.5 for the fixed-Red set (Appendix C, Table XIV). So rule sets with 26% to 67% event recall still flag every campaign within a few steps in this simulator; we did not examine why, and a real network may differ.
## RQ3: feedback and co-evolution
The interval for the co-evolved system does not overlap that of the fixed-Red baseline, so co-evolution with a varied Red improved recall in these ten runs (the replication below qualifies this). The no-feedback ablation was higher than the full system and its interval does not overlap either, so on recall the feedback loop hurt rather than helped, and at this rule cap it bought a lower false-positive rate instead (Section VI-E examines a larger cap). Table V gives the differences with bootstrap intervals: the co-evolved system is 0.23 above the fixed-Red baseline and 0.18 below the no-feedback ablation, and both intervals exclude zero. Giving Red a per-action detection signal changed little (0.43 [0.34, 0.55]; 0.07 below the standard co-evolved system, with an interval that includes zero).

## RQ4: does Red adapt?
*LLM planner.* The Red planner fell back to its default plan in one of its 160 planning calls. Over the 80 rounds of each adaptive condition it chose a stealthy scan mix in 41 or 42 rounds and a mixed one in 38, and never an aggressive one; its stated goals were balanced (38 or 39 rounds), decoy evasion (31), and stealth reconnaissance (10). The goal depended mainly on the round number: in round 1 every run chose decoy evasion, and in every later round the ten runs of a condition used at most two distinct goals. The two conditions produced nearly the same plans, so in this pilot Red's choices did not visibly depend on what Blue was doing. The per-action signal did not change this: with it, Red again chose decoy evasion in round 1 in all ten runs, used at most two goals in each later round, and fell back to the default plan once in 80 calls. We therefore describe the adaptive Red as a varied attacker whose plans follow the round number, rather than as evidence of an arms race.
*Scripted evasive Red.* Because the language-model planner did not respond to Blue, we added a scripted Red that adapts by construction. It holds four fixed plans (quiet probing, quiet push, loud push, mixed), tries each once in a random order during rounds 1 to 4, and then replays the plan with the highest reward, where reward is the share of its activity left uncaught and is zero if it achieved no successful impact. The design, the success criterion and the test were fixed before any result was seen: tune on no seeds, run once on seeds 0 to 9, score once on the test seeds, and call the claim supported only if the co-evolved recall minus the fixed-Red recall has a 95% bootstrap interval above zero without a material rise in FPR. We trained Blue with feedback against this Red and, as an ablation, Blue without feedback (ten runs each, same 4-billion-parameter model). Table VI gives the result on the seen strategies. The criterion was not met. Native recall for Blue trained against the evasive Red was 0.34 [0.22, 0.45], against 0.26 for the fixed-Red baseline; the difference was +0.087 with an interval of [-0.072, +0.241], which includes zero. FPR did not rise (0.006). The blind ablation again reached the highest recall, 0.53, and the evasive-Red Blue was 0.182 below it (interval [-0.336, -0.027]). Whether Red responded is mixed. The share of Red activity caught during training was 0.70 and 0.78 in rounds 1 and 2 and between 0.33 and 0.46 in rounds 4 to 8 with feedback, whereas without feedback it stayed between 0.60 and 0.76; but the plan chosen in the exploit rounds changed in only 8 of 30 consecutive pairs with feedback (9 of 30 without), and no plan was clearly better for Red (mean share caught 0.44 to 0.57 with feedback). So the drop in caught share mostly reflects Blue learning, and we have no evidence that a Red that learns to evade makes Blue better. We therefore do not claim that co-evolution improves detection over training against a fixed attacker.

## RQ5: robustness
To check whether the 4-billion-parameter writer drove the recall problem, we repeated the three LLM conditions with GLM-4.7-Flash (a larger mixture-of-experts model, 3-bit quantised, same prompts and settings) for three training runs each and scored them on the same frozen test episodes. Three runs are too few for firm conclusions, and the intervals in Table VII are wide; we report it as a confirmatory check only. ## Exploratory: pooling independent runs
This analysis was added after the main results were in, so it is exploratory. Individual runs miss different events, so we took the union of the rules of several independent runs of the same condition (disjoint groups of 2, 3, 5 and 10 runs) and scored it on the same frozen test episodes. A first pass on the ten main runs per condition (Fig. 9, Table 15) suggested a steep gain, but it rested on one to five groups per size, so we replicated it with ten further runs per condition on unused seeds, which gives twenty runs per condition (Table 16). Recall rose steadily with the number of runs pooled. For the co-evolved condition it went from 0.37 for one run to 0.59 for two, 0.69 for three, 0.82 for five and 0.96 for ten, while FPR grew from 0.009 to 0.018, 0.024, 0.045 and 0.080; the intervals over six, four and two groups are wide (Table 16). Ten pooled runs reach the recall of the hand-written rules (0.96) at less than half of their false-positive rate (0.080 against 0.193). The gain is not only a matter of rule count: two pooled co-evolved runs (118 rules) reached 0.59, above a single run with a cap of 20 (99 rules, 0.54), although the two intervals overlap. The first pass had also suggested that co-evolved committees beat fixed-Red committees, and the replication does not support that. With twenty runs per condition the difference in recall between co-evolved and fixed-Red committees was +0.080 [-0.105, +0.255] for groups of three and +0.057 [-0.130, +0.221] for groups of five, and the difference in FPR was -0.024 [-0.055, +0.006] and -0.027 [-0.053, +0.003], so every interval includes zero. We therefore treat the pooling gain itself as supported and any advantage of co-evolved committees over fixed-Red committees as not shown. The evasive-Red committees of the first pass behaved similarly (Table 15) but were not replicated. Pooling has a cost: about 177 rules for three runs and about 590 for ten, which must be reviewed and maintained, and about half of the rules in any single run never fire (Table XI). Adding a single LLM run to the hand-written rules changed little (recall 0.958 to between 0.968 and 0.974, FPR 0.193 to between 0.199 and 0.220), so the hand-written set's own false positives dominate.

## Baseline check: how easy is the testbed?
*Baseline check.* The hand-written set was written once and not tuned for precision, so we also fitted rule sets without an LLM on the training episodes only (seeds 0 to 9) and scored them once on the test seeds. Pruning the hand-written set to the three rules that were precise on training gave native recall 0.941 at a false-positive rate of 0.000 (0.937 and 0.000 on the unseen strategy). A greedy learner that picks up to twelve single-field rules reached recall 1.000 at 0.000, and 0.988 from a single training episode. Attacker events are 83.8% of native test events (about 5.2 per benign one), so raw precision is flattered: alerting on every event already gives 0.837. If attackers were 1 percent of events, precision at the observed recall and false-positive rate would be 0.744 for the co-evolved system, 0.726 for the fixed-Red system, 0.313 without feedback and 0.048 for the original hand-written set, against 1.000 for both fitted sets. A few fields separate the classes: every benign network event uses an ephemeral port, while 97.4 percent of attacker network events go to well-known ports. With the enriched fields blanked, the learned set still reaches recall 0.929 at 0.000, whereas the co-evolved rules fall from 0.49 to 0.19. Within a 1 percent false-positive budget, only the fitted sets and the co-evolved system qualify, and the fitted sets reach 0.94 to 1.00 recall there against 0.49. We therefore read the LLM results as what an LLM-written rule set achieves on this testbed, not as evidence that it beats simpler methods; the comparison between pooled LLM rules (about 590) and the ten hand-written rules above should be read with that in mind. A harder evaluation, with benign activity that resembles attacker activity, is the next step (results/pilot_baselines).
*What the LLM writes instead.* The LLM rules do use the discriminating field: 95 to 99 percent of their network rules constrain the destination port, and about 38 percent of all rules do. But a selection that constrains the destination port 22 and nothing else, which is how the learner's first rule starts, appears in only 1 of the 10 co-evolved runs (2 of 10 fixed-Red and 5 of 10 no-feedback runs); the LLM selections carry on average 2.1 constraints, such as zone, address, privilege or parent process. About 62 percent of all LLM rules rely on enriched fields (process name, parent process, privilege, protocol, service name) without constraining the port. These are rules that read well to an analyst, for example elevated bash under a mail-server parent, and they depend on fields that our bridge fills in, consistent with the drop to 0.19 recall when those fields are blanked (results/pilot_baselines/field_usage.md). We read this as narrow, context-rich rules that favour few false alarms over coverage; we did not test why the model writes them.
# Case Study: What the Rules Look Like
Table IX shows rules written by the co-evolved system in one training run (seed 3), taken unedited from the saved rule files. Three patterns stand out. First, the rules are readable and mostly sensible: they pair a process or port with a zone or privilege and give a short reason, in the style of a human rule. Second, they lean on the attributes the prompt examples emphasise (elevated privilege, parent process, uncommon ports), which explains the good coverage of privilege-related events and the poor coverage of exploit-spawned processes that carry ordinary attributes. # Discussion
*The trade-off.* The clearest finding is a trade-off. Rules written by a small model are quiet (few false alarms) but narrow, and they miss most of what a broad rule set catches. Hand-written and random rules sit at the opposite corner, with high recall and high false-positive rates. Performance feedback moves the writer further towards the precise corner: with feedback the false-positive rate was lower (0.006 against 0.027) and recall lower (0.49 against 0.67) at the standard cap of 12 rules per agent. The fixed-Red training curve shows the same pull, with recall decaying as rules are retired for low precision. Recall also barely differs between Red strategies, so the rule sets are limited by what they cover, not by how the attacker behaves.
*What the results support.* In the ten main runs, co-evolution with a varied attacker preserved more recall than training against one fixed attacker, without costing precision. That finding did not survive replication: ten further runs per condition on new seeds gave the opposite ordering (0.25 against 0.33), and over all twenty runs the difference was +0.076 with an interval of [-0.035, +0.184]. The other checks agree with this: with GLM-4.7-Flash the co-evolved system did not beat the fixed-Red baseline on recall, although three runs cannot settle it, a scripted evasive Red did not produce a clear benefit either, and with a larger rule cap the advantage over the fixed-Red baseline shrank to +0.145 with an interval that includes zero. The results do not support the claim that the feedback loop improves detection: the no-feedback ablation had clearly higher recall at both rule caps, and the feedback bought a lower false-positive rate at the standard cap (0.006 against 0.027) but not clearly at a cap of 20 (0.007 against 0.012). Which of the two is better depends on the cost of a missed attack against the cost of a false alert, which we do not model. We also cannot say that the gain over the fixed-Red baseline comes from the attacker responding to Blue, because the Red plans hardly depended on Blue. A possible explanation is that attacker variety, not adaptation, kept Blue exploring more patterns; the pooled-committee comparison did not separate co-evolved from fixed-Red runs once replicated (Section VI-F), so this explanation remains untested. A richer signal to Red did not change its plans either, so the 4-billion-parameter planner may be too insensitive to its memory for this design; we did not test a larger planner.
*Implications for practice.* A rule writer of this kind is useful as a proposal engine that is quiet by default, not as a replacement for broad hand-written coverage. Because about half of its rules never fire, a simple usage pattern is to prune silent rules after each round. Adding one run's rules to hand-written rules changed little, whereas pooling several independent runs raised recall a lot at a false-positive rate far below that of the hand-written rules (Section VI-F), so running several short loops and merging their rules looks more promising than relying on one run. This is a statement about LLM-written rules on this testbed, not a claim that they beat simpler methods: rule sets fitted without an LLM do better still here (Section VI-G), the hand-written set was untuned, and pooling uses about 590 rules against its ten.
# Threats to Validity and Limitations
(1) Blue is detection-only: rules label events but do not change the simulated environment, so we measure rule quality, not defence quality. (2) Part of the telemetry is synthetic, written by us from successful Red actions; we report it separately because it is easy. (3) Two quantised models were used, the second for only three runs per condition, so model-size effects are not established. (4) We used ten runs of eight rounds per condition, so intervals are still wide, and the static baseline has one run. (5) The seen Red strategies were chosen by us, and the unseen strategy may share behaviour with them. (6) The rule language has no counts or time windows, which limits what any writer, human or model, can express. (7) Ground-truth labels come from our own labeller and were not independently audited. (8) Validity of a rule is not deployability: we have not tested rules on real telemetry or measured analyst workload. (9) Several design choices (the retirement threshold, the rule cap, the prompt) were set during development, and conclusions about feedback may depend on them. (10) Ten runs per condition proved too few to settle the co-evolution comparison: ten new seeds reversed the ordering of the first ten, and only the two main conditions were replicated. (11) The testbed is easy to separate: attackers are 83.8% of native test events, benign network events all use ephemeral ports, and rule sets fitted on a few fields reach recall 0.94 to 1.00 at no false positives, so the results do not show that LLM-written rules help against harder, more overlapping traffic. (12) Native events come from only three Red actions, and part of the fields (process names, parent processes and similar) are filled in by a seeded rule that we wrote, so Blue may partly learn our data generator; with the enriched fields blanked, the co-evolved recall fell from 0.49 to 0.19 (the learned set from 1.00 to 0.93).
# Conclusion and Future Work
We built a co-evolutionary setup in which LLM agents write Sigma-style detection rules for a simulated enterprise network while an LLM planner varies the attacker. In a pilot with a small local model, the rules raised few false alarms but were incomplete: false-positive rates of at most 0.03, with native recall of 0.49 for the full system against 0.26 for a fixed attacker and 0.96 for ten hand-written rules. In the ten main runs co-evolution gave higher recall than a fixed attacker, but this did not replicate in ten further runs per condition (combined +0.076 [-0.035, +0.184]), in a three-run check with a larger model, with a scripted evasive Red, or with a larger rule budget, and performance feedback did not raise recall: an ablation without it reached 0.67 recall at a higher false-positive rate, and with a larger rule cap its recall was 0.72 with the false-positive gap largely closed. Pooling the rules of independent runs raised recall to 0.69 (three runs), 0.82 (five) and 0.96 (ten) at false-positive rates of 0.024, 0.045 and 0.080 in an exploratory analysis replicated on twenty runs per condition, although it did not show that co-evolved runs pool better than fixed-Red runs. A baseline check showed that rule sets fitted without an LLM reach recall 0.94 to 1.00 at no false positives on this testbed, whose native test events are 84% attacker events (so raw precision is flattered), and that a greedy learner finds the discriminating destination port from a single episode. The LLM rules use that port too, but mostly wrapped in narrow context and in fields our bridge filled in, and the testbed is easy to separate, so the LLM rules are not shown to beat simpler methods. The numbers are small-scale and come from one simulator; we publish them with their limitations to guide the larger study that follows.
The next steps are to test a stronger rule writer, add counts and time windows to the language, wire a Blue response into the environment so that detection can be linked to outcome, and repeat the study with an attacker whose plans demonstrably respond to being caught, for example a larger planner model or a learned adversary that is rewarded for evading the current rules.

#* Appendix E. Glossary
Table 17 explains the terms used throughout the paper in plain language.
Table: TABLE XVI. Terms used in this paper.
Term | Meaning
Detection rule | A small, readable program (a Sigma-style JSON object) that decides whether one event deserves an alert.
Event | One line of telemetry produced from the simulator, described by sixteen visible fields such as process name, port and privilege.
Native, enriched, synthetic | Events taken straight from the simulator; fields we filled in with a seeded rule because the simulator lacks them; and events we invented so that some attacker actions can be detected at all.
Hidden label | The simulator's record of whether Red caused an event. It is used for training feedback and for scoring, and is never shown to Blue or to a frozen rule.
Committee | The union of the rules of several independent training runs.
Seen and unseen Red | Attacker strategies used while developing the system, and one strategy kept back for testing only.

#* Reproducibility and Availability
Seeds, conditions, prompts, thresholds and test protocol are fixed in the experiment scripts; every number in this paper is read from the result files they write. The code, the pinned environment (Docker image and package pins), the experiment scripts and all result files, including the frozen rule sets and the cached test episodes, are in the public repository github.com/bhavitanand/co-evolutionary-purple-teaming-cage4. Re-scoring the released rule sets regenerates every table exactly; retraining calls a language model and will not reproduce the same rules.
